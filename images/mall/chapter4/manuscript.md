# The Night Guard
## Chapter Four — Across the Highway

*Days 11–16 · Maplecrest Mall*

*Complete narration manuscript. Section titles and day/location notes are not spoken. Follows Chapter Three, "The Pharmacy." American English throughout.*

### 1. Nine Percent

*Day 12 · 9:06 p.m. · The roof*

Right now I'm flying insulin across a mile and a half of the dead, and the battery says nine percent.

The drone is a little black dot of light over Parkview Apartments. I can't see it from here. I can only see what it sees, on a cracked tablet in Kip's hands, propped on a cooler on the roof of my mall.

Tenth floor. Fourth window from the left.

The window is closed.

On the screen, the curtains are drawn. There's a glow behind them, very faint. A candle, maybe. Or a flashlight pointed at the floor.

Nobody's coming to the glass.

Kip is breathing through his mouth. His thumbs are on the sticks. The drone is fighting a wind I can't feel up here, rocking a little every few seconds, and every time it rocks, the number in the corner of the screen ticks down.

Eight percent.

Under the building, on the camera, the parking lot is moving.

That's the part I keep looking at, even though I've told myself not to. The parking lot is full of them. A whole river of the dead. They followed the buzzing all the way across eight lanes of highway, and now they're standing under my daughter's window, looking up at the noise.

At the thing I sent.

Seven percent.

"Danny," Kip says. "I need to know what you want me to do."

I don't know what I want him to do.

I want my mom to open that window.

Let me back up.

### 2. Short, Short, Short

*Day 10 · Night · Summit Outdoors*

The night my daughter blinked SOS, I tried every way across that highway a man can think of while sitting on a camping bucket.

I sat at the bottom of the roof ladder with the insulin in my lap. Theo sat across from me. Pancake sat on Theo.

"Walk it," I said.

"You'd die before the first lane."

"Drive it."

"Your car's in the garage with them."

"Throw it."

He just looked at me.

"Okay," I said. "Not throw it."

We went through all of it. A rope across the highway. The hotel roof next door. Waiting for the rescue trucks, which were still, according to the radio, twenty days away.

SOS didn't mean she was out. Knowing my mom, it meant she was down to the last of it and had finally decided to tell somebody. Two days, maybe. If she was stretching it. She was always stretching it.

I'd known that for ten days. I'd done the math every single morning. And somehow it still felt like it showed up out of nowhere, like a step you miss in the dark.

Theo was quiet for a long time. Then he said:

"Fly it."

"With what?"

"The electronics store. They had drones in the window. Big ones. With cameras." He picked at the tape on his sneaker. "Kids used to test-fly them in the atrium on Saturdays. The guy let them, if their dads looked like they'd buy one."

I knew that store. I'd chased a ten-year-old out of it once for flying a drone into the fountain.

"Where is it?"

Theo didn't answer right away. He didn't have to. I could see the mall map in my head.

Upper level. North wing.

Right next door to the department store.

On her side.

"Even if we got one," Theo said, "you'd have to set it up. For that far. Batteries. Range. The thing where it won't fly over certain places." He shrugged. "I don't know how."

"Do you know who does?"

He looked at the radio sitting on top of the cooler, battery out.

"The guy with the cables."

### 3. My Turn to Call

*Day 11 · Morning*

For eleven days, the woman on the radio had called me. On day eleven, I called her.

I put the battery in at seven in the morning. I held the button down for a long time before I said anything.

"Vera."

She answered on the first try. Like she'd been sitting there with her hand on it.

"Well. Good morning, Danny."

"I need something from your side."

"I know." She said it gently. "I saw the light last night too."

I closed my eyes.

Of course she had. She'd been watching that window as long as I had.

"There's a drone in the electronics store," I said. "I need it, and I need the kid who can set it up. Kip."

A small pause.

"You've learned names. That's nice." Another pause. "What are you offering?"

I'd thought about this all night.

"Water. As much as you need. I know where there's more than either of us can drink." I didn't tell her where. "And the pharmacy. Key twelve. You can go in whenever you want."

"That's generous."

"And I'll owe you. Again."

"You do already owe me, sweetheart. That's not new money."

On the other side of the store, Theo was lying on his stomach with his chin on his arms, listening.

"What else?" I said.

"You know what else."

I did know.

"The last key," Vera said. "The one with the little red tag. You give me that, you get your drone, you get Kip, and your mother gets her medicine."

I looked at the keyring on my belt. Forty-two keys. One of them had a red plastic tag that said B3 in faded marker.

Walt had tapped it with his finger. *That one, you never use. Don't ask.*

Somebody had drilled through a wall to reach whatever it opened. People had died in that noise. Marcus had died in that noise.

"Okay," I said.

Theo sat up.

"Lovely," Vera said. "Tomorrow morning, at the fence. Bring the key. I'll bring the boy."

She clicked off.

Theo was staring at me like I'd just set the store on fire.

"You can't give her that," he said.

"I know," I said.

"Then why'd you say yes?"

I took the keyring off my belt and laid it on the floor between us.

"I didn't say which key."

### 4. Two Keys

*Day 11 · Summit Outdoors*

I gave a thief a fake key, and I hid the real one on a dog.

Walt left me a list when he retired. One sheet of paper, folded in quarters, in the inside pocket of the key pouch. Every key had a number and a store. Most of them, I knew by heart.

Number nine said: *J-2. Janitor. Lower level, by the restrooms.*

It was old and brass, the same as the B3 key. Same size. Same worn teeth, give or take. If you weren't the person who'd carried those keys every night for six years, you would not know the difference.

I worked the little red tag off the B3 key with my teeth and a pair of pliers from the camping aisle. It didn't want to come. It had been on there longer than I'd had the job.

Then I put it on key nine.

Theo watched the whole operation from his bucket.

"That's your plan," he said.

"That's my plan."

"She's going to try it."

"Eventually."

"And then she's going to know."

"Eventually."

He thought about that.

"What happens eventually?"

I didn't have a good answer. I had a bad one, which was: by then my mom would be alive, and I'd work out the rest after.

That was the same thing I'd said to him about the water. It seemed to keep working, as long as you didn't look at it directly.

The real key, the one with no tag now, sat in my hand. It looked like nothing. That was the point.

I needed somewhere to put it that wasn't me. If they ever got hold of me, the first thing anybody would check was the belt.

Pancake wandered over and sat on my foot.

He had a little red collar from the pet store. There was a plastic name tag on it, blank, because nobody had ever bought him and nobody had ever written his name.

I unbuckled the collar. Threaded the key on behind the tag, so it lay flat against the leather. Buckled it back up.

Pancake shook himself. The key made no sound at all.

Theo stared at the dog.

"Nobody checks the dog," I said.

Theo opened his mouth, closed it, and then, for the first time since I'd met him, laughed out loud.

He stopped right away, with his hand over his mouth. We both listened.

Out in the hall, Marcus rattled a gate. Paused. Moved on.

Nobody else came.

At nine, I went up on the roof. Lily's window blinked SOS again. Short, short, short. Long, long, long.

I blinked back three times.

*Hold on,* is what I meant. I don't know what it looked like from over there.

### 5. The Fence

*Day 12 · Morning · The roof*

I handed the woman who'd called the dead into my mall the key she'd been looking for, and it was the wrong one.

She was at the fence before me this time. Coat buttoned up. Price tag still on the sleeve. The big man stood behind her, the one I'd decided was a wall. His name, I'd learn later, was Boone.

Kip stood off to the side holding a black plastic case almost as big as he was.

And there were two people I hadn't seen before.

A woman in her fifties, in a gray cleaning smock, holding a mop bucket full of empty water bottles. And a young guy, early twenties, with the same face as her, carrying two more.

I knew her.

Ana. She'd cleaned the department store at night for as long as I'd worked there. Every night at three a.m., she'd wheel her cart past the security window and wave without looking up, and I'd wave back. In six years, I don't think we'd said fifty words to each other.

She looked at me through the fence now and didn't wave. She looked at Vera first, like she was checking whether she was allowed to.

"Ana and Luis," Vera said. "We found them in the stockroom on the first night, hiding behind the mattresses. They've been helping out."

Luis didn't look at anybody. He set the bottles down on the roof and stepped back.

"Key," Vera said.

She unlocked the padlock on the fence gate with her own key. I didn't ask where she'd gotten it. Then she opened the gate exactly as wide as her hand.

I put key nine in her palm, red tag and all.

She held it up to the morning light and turned it. Ran her thumb over the faded B3. For a second I was sure she knew. I was sure she could feel the difference through the metal.

Then she closed her fingers around it.

"Walt's last key," she said softly. "Do you know how long I've wanted to hold this?"

I didn't answer.

She tucked it into the inside pocket of the coat with the price tag.

"Kip," she said. "Go with Danny. Bring him back in one piece."

Kip came through the gate with his case. Boone watched him go with no expression at all.

Then Vera smiled at me, closed the gate, and locked it.

"Water by noon," she said. "Ana will collect."

I carried the case down the ladder myself, because Kip's hands were shaking too hard.

### 6. The Guy with the Cables

*Day 12 · The stockroom*

The kid who flew my drone was more scared of Vera than of the dead.

His name was Kip. He was twenty-four. He talked fast when he was nervous, which was always.

He set up on the stockroom table and opened the case. Inside was the biggest drone the electronics store sold. Gray, with four folded arms and a camera on the front like an eye.

"Range on this thing is rated for about eight miles," he said. "Which is a lie. That's in a desert, with no wind, no buildings, no radio noise. Across a city? I'd trust it for two. Maybe."

"It's a mile and a half."

"Then we're inside maybe. Love that for us."

He'd found four batteries in the store. Only two would take a full charge. He plugged them into the generator outlet behind the camping stoves, and we watched the little lights come on.

"I charged them all night on her side," he said. "This is just topping them off. They'll be full by dark."

"Flight time, about thirty minutes on a good day," he said. "Carrying weight, less. Wind, less. Me being nervous, honestly also less."

"How much weight?"

I showed him the insulin. Three boxes, wrapped in the dish towel.

He weighed them in his hand like a kid guessing the weight of a pumpkin. Then he found a foam drink cooler in the camping aisle, the kind you'd put a soda in, and fit the boxes inside so they'd stay cool and wouldn't fall apart if it landed hard.

"How do they get it off the drone?" Theo asked.

Kip jumped about a foot.

Theo was sitting on top of the storage rack, legs hanging down, where he'd been the entire time.

Kip looked at him. Then at me.

I'd known this moment was coming since I said yes on the radio. If Kip was going to work in my store, he was going to see Theo. And if he told Vera, she'd know who'd heard her drill on night one.

Kip looked at Theo for a long time.

"I didn't see anybody," he said finally. "There's nobody up there."

Then he turned back to his drone, and his hands were shaking a little worse.

"The hook," he said. "It's a release hook. You press a button, it lets go. If somebody opens the window, I fly it right up to the glass and drop it inside."

"And if nobody opens the window?"

Kip didn't say anything to that.

I didn't either.

### 7. A Light at the Window

*Day 12 · Afternoon*

To get my mom to open her window, I had to use the only language my daughter and I had.

The drone had a spotlight on the bottom. Kip showed me. On, off. On, off.

"Three times," Theo said.

I looked at him.

"That's your signal. She'd know it was you."

I'd been so busy with batteries and weight and wind, I hadn't thought about what Lily would see. Something buzzing at her window at night, in the middle of all this. My mom was the type to hit it with a broom.

But if it blinked three times.

Kip wrote it down on his hand. *3 blinks @ window.*

"She does it at nine," I said. "Every night. If we launch at nine, she'll already be at the window."

"Then we launch at nine," Kip said.

He stayed in the stockroom all afternoon, testing. He flew the drone up and down inside the store, about two feet off the floor, so quietly I couldn't believe it. Pancake chased the shadow across the carpet until Theo picked him up.

Around four, Kip finally sat down.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Sure."

"Why'd you give her the key?"

I kept my face very still.

"Because she asked."

"She doesn't ask," he said. "I've known her two years. She doesn't ask for anything. She just tells you what you're going to do, very nicely, and then you do it."

He picked at the label on a water bottle.

"I told you," he said, very quietly. "In the note. I meant it."

"I know."

"Then why'd you give it to her?"

I looked at Pancake, asleep in Theo's lap. At the little red collar.

"I didn't," I said.

Kip looked at the dog. Then at me.

He didn't ask anything else after that. But he started breathing a little easier.

### 8. Launch

*Day 12 · 9:00 p.m. · The roof*

At nine o'clock, my daughter blinked SOS, and I answered with a drone.

All four of us went up. Kip with the tablet and the controller. Theo with the spare battery tucked inside his sweatshirt to keep it warm. Me with my flashlight.

Pancake stayed downstairs with a hot dog chew toy. We'd learned our lesson.

At nine, the tenth-floor window lit up.

Short, short, short. Long, long, long. Short, short, short.

I blinked back three times. Steady. Slow.

Then I nodded to Kip.

The drone lifted off the roof with a sound like a swarm of bees in a jar. Louder than in the stockroom. Much louder. It rose over the edge of the mall and tipped forward, out over the parking lot, toward the highway.

On the tablet, I watched the mall fall away behind it.

I'd never seen it from above. It looked so small. Just a flat gray box with a dark parking lot around it. All those stores. All those keys.

Then the highway.

Eight lanes, all stopped. Cars with their doors hanging open. And between them, everywhere, standing, the dead.

Hundreds. More than I'd ever counted from the roof. The camera saw what I couldn't, all the way to the far side.

As the drone passed over them, they looked up.

First a few. Then a whole lane. Faces turning upward in the dark, one after another, following the buzz like sunflowers follow the sun.

"Uh," Kip said. "Danny."

"Keep going."

"They're—"

"I see it. Keep going."

He kept going.

Battery: eighty-four percent.

### 9. The River

*Day 12 · Over the highway*

The noise that was saving my mom was pulling every dead thing on that highway toward her door.

I could see it happening on the tablet, and I couldn't do anything about it.

Behind the drone, they were moving. Not one or two. All of them. The ones in the lanes. The ones between the cars. The ones on the shoulder by the sound wall. All shuffling the same way, all following the same buzz, filling in behind it like water running downhill.

Rule one. They follow sound.

I'd known that for twelve days. I'd built my whole life around it. I'd walked across a center court in my socks because of it.

And I'd sent the loudest thing in the city straight across the highway, at night, toward my family.

"Do I stop?" Kip whispered.

"If you stop, she doesn't get it."

"If I don't stop, they follow it all the way there."

"I know."

He looked at me.

The drone was halfway across. Below it, the river of the dead was already crossing the median, climbing over cars, coming.

"Keep going," I said.

There was a wind coming from the west. I could see it on the screen. The drone kept tilting into it, correcting, tilting again. Kip's thumbs were moving the whole time.

"Headwind," he said. "It's eating the battery."

Sixty-one percent.

Fifty-three.

The battery number was falling faster than the distance was shrinking. Even I could see that. Kip was mumbling numbers under his breath. Speed, distance, minutes.

"Is it going to make it?"

"It's going to make it there," he said.

He didn't say the rest. I heard it anyway.

Parkview came up on the screen. A tall brick building, every window dark except one, the glow on the tenth floor. I counted. I always counted.

Fourth from the left.

Twenty-two percent.

Kip took the drone up the side of the building, floor by floor. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Ten.

The curtains were closed.

### 10. I Knew It Was You

*Day 12 · 9:06 p.m. · Parkview Apartments, tenth floor*

I hadn't seen my daughter in eleven days. Then she held up a drawing to a drone.

The drone hung outside the closed window and rocked in the wind.

Nine percent.

"Blink it," I said.

Kip pressed the spotlight button. On. Off. On. Off. On. Off.

Three times.

Nothing.

Eight percent.

Seven.

Under the building, on the camera, the parking lot was filling up with them. They'd followed it the whole way. They stood in rows, faces turned up toward the buzzing on the tenth floor, the way a crowd watches fireworks.

"Danny," Kip said. "I need to know what you want me to do."

And then the curtain moved.

A small hand pulled it back.

And there she was.

Lily.

In her purple pajamas, the ones with the cats. Her hair was a mess. She was pressed right up against the glass, so close that her breath fogged it, and she was looking straight into the camera.

Straight at me.

I made some kind of noise. I don't know what. Theo grabbed my arm.

Then she held something up to the glass.

A piece of paper. A drawing, in purple marker. Wobbly capital letters, and underneath, a stick figure with a crown and a flashlight, standing on top of a gray box.

*I KNEW IT WAS YOU.*

She'd drawn it before the drone ever got there. She'd been waiting for me to come up with something. She'd been sure I would.

Behind her, the window slid open. A hand reached past Lily. An old hand, with a gold ring on it.

My mom.

Kip didn't wait for me to tell him. He nudged the drone forward, right up to the open window, and pressed the release.

The foam cooler dropped onto the windowsill.

My mom's hand closed around it and pulled it inside.

Lily pressed her drawing flat against the glass with both hands and didn't take it away.

Five percent.

### 11. Nobody Comes Back

*Day 12 · 9:09 p.m.*

The drone that saved my mom couldn't come home, so I used it to lead them away from her.

"I can't get it back," Kip said. "Not at five percent. Not into that wind."

On the screen, Lily was still at the window. My mom had pulled the curtain halfway closed behind her and was trying to get her to come away from the glass. Lily wasn't moving.

Below them, the parking lot was full.

All of those faces. Looking up.

"Fly it away," I said.

Kip glanced at me.

"Away from the building. As far as it'll go. Make them follow it."

He understood. He turned the drone away from the window, and Lily's face swung out of the picture, and I let it.

The drone dropped low over the parking lot. Low enough that the camera passed right over their heads. Twenty feet. Ten.

Kip flew it down the street, away from Parkview. Toward the old car wash on the corner. Slow, so they could keep up.

Some of them followed.

Not enough.

Three percent.

He got it past the car wash. Past a bus shelter. The battery warning was flashing red over the whole screen now.

One percent.

The picture tipped sideways and went to gray.

Up on the roof, it was very quiet.

I looked across the highway. I couldn't see the drone. I couldn't see the parking lot. All I could see was the tenth floor, fourth window from the left, with a light behind the curtain.

And then the light blinked.

One. Two. Three.

I blinked back. My hands were shaking so badly that the beam jumped all over the sky.

Kip sat down on the cooler.

Theo sat down next to him.

Nobody said anything for a while.

The insulin was across the highway. My mom was going to be okay. My daughter had seen me, sort of, for the first time in eleven days.

And there were a few hundred dead people standing around her building, looking up at the place where the noise had been.

I'd done that.

I'd opened a door, and they'd all come through it.

### 12. Side by Side

*Days 13–15 · Maplecrest Mall*

For three days, I lived next to the woman who'd called the dead into my mall, and she made pancakes.

That's the part nobody would believe.

Vera was good at it. At all of it. By day thirteen, she had a schedule. Water runs at noon, when Marcus's loop was at the far end. Pharmacy trips in pairs. Lights out at ten. She talked to Ana and Luis like a nice boss at a good job. She remembered that Luis didn't eat eggs. She found a griddle in the kitchen store and made pancakes from a box on a camp stove, on the roof, for everybody, and she brought a plate to the fence for me.

They were good pancakes.

I hated how good they were.

Every afternoon, Ana came to the fence with the empty bottles, and every afternoon I filled them from what Theo and I carried back from the fountain. I never showed them the route. I never told them where the water came from.

On the second day, Ana said, "Thank you, Danny," very quietly, when Vera was out of earshot.

It was the most she'd ever said to me.

On the third day, she said, "Luis wants to know if you have a deck of cards."

I gave her three.

Kip stayed on my side. Vera said it was so he could keep an eye on the second drone battery. I think she said it so she'd have someone in my store.

He knew that. I knew that. He knew I knew.

But every night, he sat on the floor of the stockroom with Theo and taught him how to take a radio apart. And every night at nine, all three of us climbed to the roof and watched the tenth floor.

Three blinks. Every night.

She was okay. My mom was okay.

The dead were still standing around the building. Fewer every night, Kip said, squinting through a pair of binoculars from the camping aisle. But still there.

On the night of day fifteen, after the blinks, Kip didn't go downstairs.

He sat down on the edge of the roof hatch and looked across at the department store.

"There's some things I should tell you," he said.

### 13. The Fourth Man

*Day 15 · Night · The roof*

The kid who flew my drone told me how they'd planned to rob my mall for two years, and how they'd left one of their own outside.

"There's a legend," Kip said. "You know what this land was before the mall?"

I didn't.

"A bank. Tri-County Savings and Loan. It went under in nineteen-eighty-one. The story is that when it failed, there was money that never got found. A lot of it. And when they built Maplecrest on top of the old foundation, the vault was still down there. Under the new building. Sealed behind a door they never put on any map."

"B3," I said.

He nodded.

"Vera heard the story from her uncle, who heard it from a guy who poured concrete for the mall. She spent two years on it. Floor plans. Permits. Old newspapers. She got me a job on the cleaning contractor's IT team so I could get into the camera system."

He looked at his hands.

"That's how she knew your name. I tapped the cameras, months ago. She's watched you on your rounds since last spring. She knows which doors you check twice. She knows Walt's name because I pulled the old staff files for her."

I thought about every night I'd walked this building, alone, thinking nobody was watching.

"She knew about the key," Kip said. "She just couldn't get it off you. So we were going to go through the wall instead. Drill down. That was the plan. Get in, get to the door, get out by morning."

"And then they came."

"And then they came." He swallowed. "Because of us. Because of the drill. I knew it was too loud. I said it was too loud. She said one more minute."

"Theo told me," I said.

He nodded slowly, like he'd guessed.

"There were four of us," he said. "Not three. There was Dale. He was on the loading dock, keeping lookout. When they started coming in, he ran back to the department store door and he banged on it. He was yelling for us."

Kip's voice got very small.

"Vera locked it. She stood there with her hand on the lock and said, *We can't open it. Not with them out there.* And we listened to him until we couldn't."

I didn't say anything.

I couldn't.

I was standing on a roof, and I was also standing on the other side of a steel gate, two weeks ago, with a key in my hand, listening to my best friend ask me to open it.

*We can't open it. Not with them out there.*

I knew that move.

I'd made it too.

Kip looked up at me.

"You're not her," he said. Like he'd read my mind.

"I locked the same gate," I said.

"You sat with him," Kip said. "For forty minutes. She didn't sit with Dale. She went to bed."

### 14. My Turn

*Day 16 · Maplecrest Mall*

I gave a thief a fake key, and on day sixteen, she found out.

It took her four days to reach the door.

Kip told me later how they did it. The stairs down to the lower level were behind the pretzel place, right where they'd drilled on night one. The hallway down there was packed with the dead who'd followed the noise. So every day, Vera and Boone cleared it a little more, using road flares and the noise of a battery-powered alarm clock to pull them up and out into the center court.

Slow. Patient. Like she'd done everything for two years.

On the afternoon of day sixteen, the center court got very crowded. I watched it from my gate. More of them than I'd seen in days, all milling around, all turned toward the pretzel place.

Kip was sitting beside me.

"She's down there," he said.

I didn't ask how he knew. He'd lived with her for two years.

An hour went by. Then another.

Then Marcus's radio, battery in, sitting on the cooler, crackled.

"Danny."

Her voice was exactly the same as always. Warm. Calm. Amused.

"I'm standing in front of a door," she said. "It's very old. Very heavy. No sign on it. I've been waiting for this door for two years."

A pause.

"I've got your key in it."

I didn't answer.

"And it doesn't turn," she said.

Theo was on the rack. Kip was beside me. Pancake was asleep on the sleeping bag with his collar on, and my hand was on his back.

"So I walked around the corridor," Vera said. "Tried a few other doors. Just to see."

Another pause. Longer.

"This key opens a mop closet, Danny."

I looked at the radio.

I could hear her breathing on the other end. Steady. Not angry. That was the worst part. She didn't sound angry at all.

The silence went on for a long time.

Then she said:

"Okay."

And:

"My turn."

The radio clicked off.

Kip had gone completely white.

Across the store, Theo looked down at me from the rack. At the gate. At the dog with the key on his collar.

For sixteen days, I'd been afraid of what she might do if she didn't get what she wanted.

Now I was going to find out.

What does a woman who'll lock a man out to the dead do when you lie to her?
