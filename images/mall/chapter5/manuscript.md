# The Night Guard
## Chapter Five — The Fall of the King

*Days 17–25 · Maplecrest Mall*

*Complete narration manuscript. Section titles and day/location notes are not spoken. Follows Chapter Four, "Across the Highway." American English throughout.*

### 1. One Key

*Day 23 · Night · The security office*

Three weeks ago, I had a key to every store in this mall. Tonight I have one, and it's tied to a dog's collar.

The dog is asleep on my foot.

We're in the security office, upstairs, behind a steel door with a keypad. I know the code. I'm the only one who does. That's the only reason we're alive.

The screens on the wall are black. The generator died yesterday. There's no light in here except a camping lantern turned down so low it's more of a suggestion.

Theo's sitting in the corner with his knees pulled up. He's holding a pair of glasses that aren't his. He hasn't put them down in six hours.

Ana and her son are asleep against the filing cabinets. Or pretending to be.

Out in the hallway, they're everywhere now. I can hear them through the door. Shuffling. Bumping. A hand dragging along the wall, back and forth, like somebody looking for a light switch.

Every few minutes, the radio on the desk crackles. It's the security channel. I don't pick it up.

I'm done picking it up.

I keep thinking about the doors. All the doors I opened in the last three weeks. The gate for Theo. The cage for the dog. The fence for her.

Every one of them felt like the right thing. Every one of them felt like I was finally becoming somebody.

Pancake sighs in his sleep and stretches out across my boot. The key on his collar clinks once against the floor.

I put my hand over it.

Let me back up.

### 2. Your Turn

*Day 17 · Summit Outdoors*

The woman I'd lied to came back the next morning with pancakes, and that scared me more than anything she could have said.

After the radio clicked off on day sixteen, I didn't sleep. I sat on a camping bucket facing the gate with an aluminum baseball bat across my knees, like a man in a movie who hasn't figured out yet that he's in a different movie.

Kip sat up with me. He didn't say much. Once, around three in the morning, he said, "She doesn't yell," and then he didn't explain, and I didn't ask.

Nothing happened.

At eight in the morning, there was a knock on the roof hatch.

Theo went up the rack so fast I didn't see him go. Kip turned white. I climbed the ladder with the bat.

It was Vera. Alone, on her side of the fence. Holding a paper plate with a stack of pancakes on it.

She was laughing.

"A mop closet," she said. "A mop closet, Danny. I walked all the way down there with Boone carrying two road flares, and I stood in front of the most important door of my life, and I put in a key that opens buckets."

I didn't say anything.

"Honestly? That was good." She wiped her eyes. "I mean it. I would have done exactly the same thing."

She pushed the plate through the gap at the bottom of the fence.

"Keep the key," she said. "Keep all of them. I'm not going to fight you for a piece of metal in the middle of the end of the world. That's not who I want to be."

"Then what was 'my turn'?"

She smiled.

"This," she said. "My turn to say sorry. I shouldn't have made you trade your mother's life for it. That wasn't fair."

She went back inside.

I stood on the roof holding a plate of pancakes and a baseball bat.

Downstairs, Kip took one look at the pancakes and said, "Don't."

"Don't eat them?"

"Don't believe her."

I ate them anyway. They were really good.

I believed her a little.

### 3. Hello, Theo

*Day 18 · The roof*

The woman on the radio said hello to the kid in my ceiling by name, and she'd known about him for months.

Ana came for water at noon, like always. This time, Vera came with her.

Theo had started helping with the handoffs. He was quieter than me on the ladder and better at carrying. He kept his hood up, and he kept behind me, and he never looked at the department store side.

Vera looked right at him.

"Hello, Theo," she said.

He froze with a bottle in each hand.

I hadn't told her his name. Kip hadn't told her. I was sure of that.

"Don't look so worried," she said. "I've known about you since August. You steal pretzels from the kiosk after close and you leave the wrappers in the planter by the elevator. I used to watch you on the cameras when I couldn't sleep."

Theo didn't say anything. His jaw was tight.

"I never told anybody," Vera said. "Not security. Not your mall cop here. Not anybody. I figured you had your reasons."

She smiled at him. It was a kind smile. I've thought about it a lot since. I think, in that moment, it might even have been real.

"You're safe here," she said. "That's all I wanted to say."

She went back inside.

Theo set the bottles down very carefully, and then he went down the ladder without a word.

I found him later in the stockroom, sitting on top of the rack in the dark.

"She called them," he said. "On night one. She stood there and told them to keep drilling."

"I know."

"Then why are you being nice to her?"

I didn't have a good answer.

"Because she's being nice to us," I said.

He looked at me like I'd just said the stupidest thing he'd ever heard.

Which, looking back, I had.

### 4. The Grocery Stockroom

*Day 18 · Afternoon · The lower level*

I opened a door for the woman who called the dead into my mall, and it was the best day I'd had in three weeks.

The grocery store was on the lower level, by the north entrance. Its stockroom had a steel door, and the steel door had a lock, and the lock was key twenty-five.

Behind it, according to Kip, who'd seen the delivery logs, was four weeks of canned food for a store that would never open again.

Vera suggested it. She suggested it the way she suggested everything, like it had just occurred to her, like you could say no.

"Your key, my people," she said through the fence. "Nobody carries more than they can carry quietly. We split it down the middle."

I said yes.

We went at noon, when Marcus's loop was at the far end. Me in front with the key. Boone behind me, silent. Then Ana and Luis with laundry carts from the department store, the wheels wrapped in towels.

Vera came last. She didn't carry anything. She watched the hall, and every time one of them turned toward us, she'd touch somebody's arm, and they'd stop, and wait, and go again. She was good at it. She was better at it than me.

The stockroom door opened without a squeak. I'd oiled it that morning with chain lube from the bike aisle.

Inside: shelves of cans to the ceiling. Soup. Beans. Peaches. Three pallets of bottled water. A whole wall of peanut butter.

Luis made a sound like he was about to cry. Ana put her hand on his back.

We made four trips. Nobody got hurt. On the last one, Vera stood in front of the peanut butter wall with her hands on her hips and said, very seriously, "I'd like to be buried here."

I laughed.

I didn't mean to. It just came out. Out loud, in a stockroom, three weeks into the end of the world.

Vera grinned at me.

"There he is," she said.

That night on the roof, she made soup on the camp stove for both sides of the fence. Ana passed bowls through the gap. Luis taught Theo a card game. Kip ate standing up, watching everyone.

It was the first time since night one that the mall felt like a place people lived.

I'd done that. I'd opened a door, and it was good.

I remember thinking that.

### 5. The Open Gate

*Day 20 · The roof*

On day twenty, I unlocked the fence between us, and I left it open.

Technically, Vera unlocked it. She had the padlock key. But she asked me first, and I said yes.

"It's silly," she said. "Passing soup through a fence like we're in prison. We're all in the same mall."

She wasn't wrong. Every handoff took twice as long through that gap. Every time Ana came for water, she had to crouch down on the gravel like she was feeding something at a zoo.

And there was the other thing. The thing I kept coming back to.

Ana and Luis. They'd been in the department store with Vera's crew for three weeks. They called her Ms. Vera. They waited for her to nod before they ate. I didn't like how they looked at her. Like people at a job they couldn't afford to lose.

If the fence was open, they could come to my side whenever they wanted.

That's what I told myself.

Theo found out when he came up the ladder that night and saw the gate standing open, and Luis sitting on our side of the roof shuffling a deck of cards.

He went straight back down.

I followed him.

"She called them," he said. He wasn't whispering. "She called them, Danny. They came in because of her. Marcus died because of her."

"I know."

"Then why does she get to come over here?"

"Because I can't keep locking every door," I said. "That's what I did on night one. I locked the door and I sat behind it, and I listened to my best friend die. I'm not doing that anymore."

He looked at me for a long time.

"That's not the same," he said.

"It feels the same."

"It's not the same."

Kip was sitting on the floor of the stockroom with his back against the wall. He'd heard all of it. He didn't say anything.

But when I went back up to the roof, I saw him take the screwdriver out of his kit and slide it into his sleeve.

### 6. She Sounds Worth a Lot

*Day 20 · 9:00 p.m. · The roof*

I told the woman on the radio everything about my daughter, because I thought we were friends.

At nine, we all watched the tenth floor together. Both sides of the fence. Kip with his binoculars. Ana with her arms wrapped around herself. Luis, Theo, Vera, me.

The light came on.

One. Two. Three.

I blinked back. Behind me, somebody started clapping, very softly, and then they all were. Even Boone. Two slow claps.

I'm not going to pretend that didn't feel good.

Afterward, Vera sat down next to me on the edge of the roof.

"Tell me about her," she said.

So I did.

I told her about Saturdays at the pet store. How Lily named Pancake, and how she'd sit on the floor in front of his cage and tell him about her week. I told her about the drawing taped to the monitor in the security office, the one with the crown. MY DAD IS THE KING OF THE MALL. I told her Lily was seven. That she was smart. That she figured out the flashlight on her own the night the power went out, because nobody had to tell her.

I hadn't talked about Lily to anyone in twenty days. Not like that. It was like opening a window.

Vera listened to all of it. She didn't interrupt once.

"I never had kids," she said finally. "I always thought there'd be time."

"There still might be."

She smiled at that. Looked across the highway at the tenth floor.

"She sounds like she's worth a lot," Vera said.

I thought she meant it as a compliment.

I thought about that sentence a lot later. Probably more than she did.

### 7. Unless You Have a Kid

*Day 21 · Summit Outdoors*

The radio told us rescue wasn't coming. Unless you had a kid.

For three weeks, the camping radio played the same thirty seconds. Shelter in place. Rescue teams will reach every area within thirty days. Over and over. I'd stopped hearing it. It was just a sound, like the hum of the freezers.

On day twenty-one, at six in the morning, it changed.

Kip heard it first. He was on watch. He came down the ladder and shook me awake without saying anything, and he turned the radio up as loud as he dared.

A different voice. Faster. Tired.

*Rescue teams will no longer reach all areas. Civilians who are able should proceed to Fort Halvorsen, ninety miles north on Route Nine. Evacuation flights will continue for nine more days. Children and their guardians will be evacuated first.*

Then it started over.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Thirty days. That's what we'd been counting toward. Every morning since day one. Rescue was thirty days away, and I could do thirty days, and I'd done diets longer than that.

Rescue wasn't coming.

You had to go to it. Ninety miles. Through all of that.

Ana sat down on a camping chair and put her face in her hands. Luis stood behind her.

Theo looked at me.

"How far's ninety miles?" he asked.

"Far," I said.

Then I thought about the last line.

*Children and their guardians will be evacuated first.*

Lily was a child. If somebody could get her to that fort, she'd be on the first plane out. My mom too, as her guardian.

If somebody could get her there.

I was a mile and a half away, on the wrong side of eight lanes of the dead, and I couldn't even get myself across.

When I climbed up to the roof an hour later, Vera was already there, sitting on a cooler on her side of the open gate. She had a pen in her hand.

She'd written something on the back of her wrist.

*Fort H. Rt 9. 9 days.*

She saw me looking, and she pulled her sleeve down over it.

"Well," she said. "That changes things."

### 8. Lights Out

*Day 22 · Maplecrest Mall*

When the generator died, the smell of three weeks of thawing food rang a dinner bell for the whole city.

It went at four in the afternoon.

I didn't hear it stop. I just noticed the silence afterward. A hum I'd stopped noticing three weeks ago was suddenly gone, and the emergency lights in the hall went out one by one, like somebody walking down the corridor flipping switches.

The cameras went dark. The pharmacy fridge went dark. The food court freezers went dark.

Three weeks of fuel. Walt had told me that, years ago, on my first night. *Generator's good for about three weeks, kid. If you ever need it longer than that, we've all got bigger problems.*

By the next morning, you could smell the food court from the roof.

Meat, mostly. Thousands of pounds of it, thawing in steel boxes behind the counters. Burger patties. Chicken. The orange chicken from the Chinese place that Marcus said tasted like a candle.

It's hard to describe what three weeks of rotting meat smells like. Mostly it smells like a warning.

And they could smell it too.

From the roof, with Kip's binoculars, I watched them come. Out of the neighborhoods behind the mall. Out of the parking lot. Off the highway, in ones and twos, then in dozens. All drifting the same way, toward the north wall, where the food court's big glass windows faced the street.

They pressed against it. All together. The way they'd leaned on my gate.

Glass gives. Steel holds. That was the second rule. I'd known it since night one.

The food court had more glass than anything in the mall.

That afternoon, Vera called a meeting on the roof. Both sides.

"We should leave," she said. "Soon. Before that window goes."

"And go where?" I said.

"Fort Halvorsen."

"Ninety miles. Through that?"

"In a truck," she said. "There's a delivery truck in the loading dock. Boone checked it. It's got half a tank."

Ana looked at Luis. Luis looked at the ground.

"My family's across the highway," I said.

Vera looked at me for a long moment.

"I know they are, sweetheart," she said.

### 9. Boone

*Day 22 · Night · Summit Outdoors*

I let her in, and she took every key I own.

I'd been sleeping on the floor next to the gate since the generator died. Bat beside me. Keyring on my belt. Pancake curled up against my stomach.

I woke up because I couldn't breathe.

There was a hand over my mouth. A big hand, the size of a dinner plate, and a knee on my chest, and when I opened my eyes in the dark, all I could see was a gray beard.

Boone.

He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. I tried to sit up, and it was like trying to lift a car.

The roof hatch was open. I could see stars through it. They'd come down the ladder, through the stockroom, while I was asleep. Through the gate I'd left open.

A flashlight clicked on, pointed at the floor.

Vera crouched down next to me.

"I'm so sorry about this," she said. And the worst part is, I think she meant it. "I really did like the soup nights."

She unclipped the keyring from my belt. Forty keys, jingling in the dark. She already had the forty-first. The one that opens buckets.

Pancake woke up. He stood up on my stomach and looked at her and started to growl, very low.

Vera reached out and scratched him behind his flat ear.

"Hello, Pancake," she said softly. "Aren't you a good boy."

Her fingers were an inch from his collar.

He stopped growling. He leaned into her hand. The key lay flat against the leather, behind the blank name tag, and I couldn't breathe, and not only because of Boone.

She stood up.

"My turn," she said.

They went back up the ladder. Boone went last. He took his hand off my mouth, and his knee off my chest, and he looked at me for exactly one second. I couldn't read his face. I don't think he wanted me to.

Then the hatch closed, and I heard the fence gate on the roof bang shut behind them.

Theo came down the rack. He'd watched the whole thing from the ceiling.

Kip came out of the stockroom, holding the screwdriver.

"I'm sorry," Kip said. "I didn't hear them. I'm sorry."

I sat up. My ribs hurt. I put my hand on Pancake's collar and felt the key under my fingers.

One key.

That was all I had left.

### 10. Forty-One Keys

*Day 23 · Morning*

She tried forty-one keys on a door, and I listened to every one of them fail.

She called at seven.

I almost didn't answer. Then I realized she wasn't calling for me to answer. She'd left her button down. She wanted me to hear.

"Key one," she said.

A scrape. A click. Nothing.

"Key two."

They were at the door. B3. Down on the lower level, behind the pretzel place, in the hallway they'd spent four days clearing. I could hear Boone breathing. I could hear the echo of a small concrete room.

"Key three."

I sat on the floor with my back against the gate, and Theo sat next to me, and Kip sat next to him. Pancake was in my lap. My hand was on his collar.

"Key nine," Vera said. "The mop closet. Just to be thorough."

Scrape. Click. Nothing.

"Key eleven. Key twelve. That's your pharmacy, isn't it? Funny."

She took her time. Patient. Like everything she'd ever done. She tried each one twice.

"Key thirty-one."

My gate key.

"Key thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty."

A long pause.

"Key forty-one."

Scrape. Click.

Nothing.

The radio was quiet for so long I thought the battery had died.

Then she said, very softly:

"Forty-one keys, Danny. Where's forty-two?"

I didn't say anything.

"I counted them on the cameras, you know. Every night for months. Forty-two keys on that ring. And there's a little brass one missing. The one with no tag on it now."

Pancake shifted in my lap.

"You hid it," she said. "Of course you did."

She didn't sound angry. She sounded tired, and a little impressed.

Then, behind her voice, very far away, I heard something.

A long, low sound. Like ice on a lake in March.

Kip stood up.

"That's the glass," he said.

### 11. The North Glass

*Day 23 · Noon*

When the glass gave, the kid who saved me and the kid who flew my drone were on the wrong side of the mall.

That was my fault too.

An hour before, when Vera was still down at the door, Theo had come to me with an idea.

"Her camp's empty," he said. "She took Boone. Ana and Luis are on the roof. Nobody's in the department store."

"So?"

"So Kip's gear is over there. His drone stuff. The radio that reaches farther than yours." He looked at me. "If we're going to get across to Lily, we need it."

I said no.

He said Kip knew exactly where it was, and the ceiling route came out right above the department store's stockroom, and it would take twenty minutes.

I said no again.

Then I said yes. Because we needed that gear. Because I'd started saying yes to things. Because that's who I'd decided I was now.

Kip went with him. Theo knew the route. Kip knew the gear.

They'd been gone fifteen minutes when the glass went.

I heard it from Summit Outdoors. Not a crash. More like a long, wet crack, and then a sound like rain on a tin roof, and then nothing, and then everything.

They came in through the food court. Hundreds of them. More than I'd ever seen in one place, pushing through the broken frame, filling the court, spilling into the halls in every direction. The smell of the food court came in with them, rolling down the corridor like heat out of an oven.

The center court filled in about a minute.

My gate held. Steel holds.

But the department store was on the other end of that hallway. And its front was glass too.

I grabbed the radio.

"Kip. Theo. Where are you?"

Static.

"Kip."

Then Kip's voice, out of breath, very close to the microphone.

"Stockroom. Department store. We've got the gear. We're going up."

"Then go up."

"Danny." I could hear something behind him. A thudding. The sound of a lot of hands on glass. "Danny, the rack's too short. He can reach. I can't."

### 12. Kip

*Day 23 · The department store stockroom*

The guy who flew my drone got a kid into the ceiling, and there was nobody left to lift him.

I could hear all of it.

I could hear Theo arguing. His voice high and fast, saying no, saying they'd find another way, saying there was a desk, they could push the desk over.

I could hear Kip saying, very calmly, "There's no time for the desk."

I could hear the department store's front windows give. Not loud. Just a long, slow crack, and then a lot of footsteps that weren't walking quite right.

"Put your foot in my hands," Kip said.

"No."

"Theo. Put your foot in my hands."

A scuffle. A grunt. The sound of a ceiling tile sliding back.

"He's up," Kip said into the radio. "Danny, he's up. Theo's up."

"Kip, give me your hand," Theo said, somewhere above him. "Give me your hand."

"You can't lift me, buddy."

"Give me your hand!"

There was a pause. Kip was breathing hard.

"Danny," he said. "Tell my mom it wasn't her fault I took this job. She'll think it was."

"Kip—"

"And tell Theo to put the tile back."

Then there was a lot of noise, all at once.

Then the radio was just static.

I sat in Summit Outdoors with the radio in my hand and listened to the static until Theo's voice came through it, very small and very far away.

"I put the tile back."

It took him two hours to come home through the ceiling. He came down the rack slowly, like an old man.

He had a backpack full of radio gear on his back. And in his hand, he had a pair of glasses with tape on one arm.

Kip's glasses.

He didn't say anything. He sat down on his bucket and held them.

I didn't say anything either.

There wasn't anything in the world to say.

### 13. The Loading Dock

*Day 23 · Afternoon*

She left us all behind in a mall full of the dead, and she took the truck.

I heard the engine from the roof.

I'd gone up because there was nowhere else to go. Ana and Luis were still up there, huddled against the fence, where they'd been when the glass broke. Luis had his arms around his mother.

The truck started on the third try. A big white delivery truck, backed into the loading dock behind the department store. Its engine was the loudest thing I'd heard in three weeks.

Every head on that side of the mall turned toward it.

The dock door went up with a scream of metal. The truck pulled out fast and smashed through the chain barrier at the end of the ramp. It pushed straight through the ones on the ramp, and it didn't slow down.

I could see two people in the cab.

Vera, in the passenger seat, in her nice oatmeal coat.

Boone, driving.

They went around the back of the mall, across the parking lot, up the access road toward the highway. The engine noise dragged a whole crowd of the dead after it, out of the parking lot and onto the road.

The radio on my belt clicked.

"I'm sorry, Danny," Vera said. "Truly. You should have given me the key."

Then:

"Take care of that dog."

Then nothing. The radio went quiet, and stayed quiet, and the truck's taillights disappeared behind the sound wall on the highway.

Ana was crying. Luis was staring at the empty loading dock.

"She left us," he said. "She said we were going with her. She said Fort Halvorsen."

I got them down the ladder. I got Theo and the dog. I got the radio gear and the textbook and the last of the water we could carry.

Then I took them up through the ceiling, the way Theo taught me, to the security office.

Steel door. Keypad. Six digits. I'd typed them a thousand times.

Inside, I closed the door and listened to it lock.

And I sat down on the floor.

Marcus. Kip. Every door I'd opened. The cage, the gate, the fence. Every single time, somebody paid for it.

I'd spent my whole life locking doors and walking away. I'd decided that was cowardice. For three weeks, I'd tried to be the other kind of person.

The other kind of person got people killed.

Lock it and walk away.

That's what I did.

### 14. No Light

*Days 23–25 · Nine o'clock*

My daughter has blinked I love you every night for twenty-two days. Then, nothing.

For two days, I didn't answer anybody.

The radio crackled on the desk. Strangers, sometimes, from far away, on the security channel. A man asking if anyone could hear him. A woman reading out names. I didn't pick it up.

Theo tried to talk to me twice. The first time, he said, "Kip would've—" and couldn't finish. The second time, he said, "Marcus said the king's still in his castle," and I turned toward the wall until he stopped.

Ana took care of all of us. She made cold soup out of cans. She cleaned Theo's leg, which had healed but still looked angry. She talked to Pancake in Spanish when she thought nobody was listening.

I sat by the door.

The security office has a hatch to the roof, for the radio antenna. At nine o'clock on day twenty-three, I climbed up.

I found Parkview. Tenth floor. Fourth window from the left.

Nine o'clock.

Nothing.

Nine-oh-one. Nine-oh-five.

Nine-fifteen.

Nothing.

I told myself she was asleep. I told myself the flashlight batteries had died. I told myself the dead around the building were keeping my mom away from the window.

On day twenty-four, I went up again.

Nine o'clock. Nothing.

I stood up there until midnight, blinking. Three times. Three times. Three times. Into the dark. Over and over, until my thumb hurt and the beam got weak.

Nothing came back.

On day twenty-five, I almost didn't go.

Theo came up the hatch behind me. He didn't say anything. He just stood there next to me, holding Kip's glasses, looking at the same dark window.

Nine o'clock.

Nothing.

Nothing.

Nothing.

In twenty-five days, through the power going out, through the dead filling the parking lot, through her grandmother running out of insulin, through all of it, my daughter has never missed a night.

Not once.

Until now.

I stood on that roof with my flashlight in my hand and Theo beside me and one key left in the whole world, hanging from the collar of a sleeping dog.

Where was she?
