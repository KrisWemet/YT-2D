# Chapter 3 — Part 2

My dead best friend walks the same loop every twenty-six minutes. Tomorrow I'm going to use him.

I sat in my old chair and watched the screens for two hours.

Camera nine was the center court. A hundred of them, give or take, standing around the fountain. Most didn't move at all. A few swayed, like people waiting for a bus that was running late.

Camera eleven was the hallway past the pharmacy. The pet store was right next door, with its lights off. I could just make out the cages at the back. Lily's puppy had been in there every Saturday for a year. I didn't let myself think about that.

Camera twelve was the service corridor that ran behind both stores. Empty. Walt used to say: always know your second door.

The department store cameras were all black. Every one of them said NO SIGNAL. On night one, they'd been working fine. I wrote that down.

Then Marcus came around the corner on camera four.

I started the stopwatch on my phone. Two pulls on the shoe store gate. Two on the bank. Two on the candle store. Every gate on the lower level, in the same order he'd checked them every night for a year. Past my store. Past the food court. All the way around.

Twenty-six minutes. I timed him again. Twenty-six minutes.

At the food court, he always spent longer. Those were the big double gates, and they stuck. Four pulls. Six. Every time he did it, every head around the fountain turned toward him. For about three minutes, every one of them was looking the other way.

Three minutes. That was the whole plan.

Noon was better than night. The skylights would show me every face and every piece of broken glass. The pump room door opened onto the court beside the pretzel place. From there to the pharmacy door was sixty steps. I counted them on the screen.

Sixty steps on one loop. Wait inside for the next. Sixty steps back.

I took the book and went home through the ceiling before dark. Theo was asleep. I took the marker from my shift log and drew a line around the red on his leg, right where it stopped.

At midnight, I checked it with my flashlight. The red was past the line.

I've been the night guard at this mall for six years. Today, for the first time, I'm walking on the floor with the dead.

I spent the morning getting ready like it was a job interview.

First, the boots. I tried on every pair in my size and walked them across the stockroom tile. Hiking boots are made to grip. Grip squeaks. In the end, I put on two pairs of thick wool socks, the expensive kind I'd never have bought for myself. The tile felt cold through them. It didn't make a sound.

I packed an empty backpack with a towel in the bottom, so nothing inside would rattle. I switched off my radio and zipped it into my jacket. I wrapped the keyring in a sock again. Then I took two keys off it and clipped them to my belt, where I could reach them without looking. Twenty-six, the pharmacy. Twenty-seven, the pet store next door. They shared a back corridor. Always know your second door.

Theo was awake, sort of. His face was shiny with sweat. I showed him the line on his leg, and where the red had gone past it.

"It's winning," I said. "So I'm going."

He tried to sit up. "On the floor?"

"Just for sixty steps."

He looked at me for a long time with those glassy eyes. Then he said the same thing he'd said in the ceiling. "Don't put your foot where you think the floor is."

"It's actually floor this time."

"Then put it where you know it is."

I left him a bottle of water and the last of the painkillers, and I climbed the rack.

The route to the fountain was Theo's. Across the cinema ceiling, through the service door with key seventeen, along the walkway, over the supports at his better crossing. This time there was nobody in front of me. Just a hundred people below, and Marcus somewhere on his loop.

At the column, I climbed down the maintenance ladder into the pump room. Eight thousand gallons sat there in the dark. The door to the court had a thumb latch on the inside. I put my ear against it.

Feet shuffled past on the other side. Somebody breathed through a mouth that didn't close.

I waited for the rattle. Five minutes. Ten. I counted my breaths so I'd stop listening to them.

Then, far away at the end of the hall: two pulls on a gate. Then four. Then six.

The food court gates. I counted to ten, so every head would have time to turn.

Then I opened the door.

Walking through a crowd of the dead is easy. You just can't make a sound.

The first thing I noticed was the smell. From the ceiling, it was a smell. Down here, it was a wall.

The second thing was how normal they looked from the side. A man with reading glasses pushed up on his head. A woman holding a phone that died a week ago. A kid in a soccer jersey, number nine. They weren't doing anything. They were just standing there with their faces turned toward the food court, listening to Marcus.

I stepped out and eased the door shut behind me, so the latch wouldn't click.

One step. Two. The tile was cold through the wool.

I'd memorized the path on the screen. Left of the bench. Between the planter and the man in the brown coat. Around the broken glass by the cookie stand, because a sock on glass is a sound, and then it's a lot of blood.

Ten steps. Fifteen.

Marcus pulled the food court gates again. Every head stayed pointed that way.

At twenty steps, I passed the fountain. My boot was lying in the bottom, on its side, in the coins. I'd stood here a hundred times on my rounds. I'd fished a kid's sneaker out of that fountain once. I didn't stop.

At thirty, the man in the brown coat turned his head.

Not toward me. Just a little, like he'd heard his name across a parking lot. I stopped with one foot in the air and held it there. My leg started to shake. I could see the side of his face, gray and slack, and one ear with a little silver hoop in it.

Two pulls, far away. He turned back toward the sound.

I put my foot down.

Forty steps. Fifty. The pharmacy came up in front of me, glass from the floor to the ceiling. It was the only store on the court without a steel gate. Somebody in an office had decided gates looked unfriendly in front of a pharmacy. The door had one lock, down by the floor.

Sixty steps.

I knelt, slid key twenty-six into the lock, and turned it as slowly as I've ever turned anything.

It still clicked.

In all that silence, it sounded like somebody dropping a plate. Behind me, I heard feet shift on the tile.

Somebody had turned around.

I broke into a pharmacy with a hundred dead people standing outside the glass.

The one who'd turned was the cleaner. She was standing by her cart with her yellow wet-floor sign folded under one arm, the way she'd carried it every night for years. She was looking right at the pharmacy door. Right at me, on my knees in front of it.

I didn't breathe. She didn't move.

Then Marcus pulled the food court gates again. She turned away and pushed her cart three feet into the wall by the restrooms. Then she did it again.

I slipped inside and locked the door behind me.

The pharmacy was dim. Light came through the glass in long stripes and lay across the aisles. Sunscreen. Cough drops. A cardboard woman smiling and holding up vitamins. Behind the counter, the medicine sat on shelves in alphabetical order, and I'd like to thank whoever was in charge of that.

C. I read the labels with my nose almost touching them. Cefdinir. Cefuroxime. Cephalexin, five hundred milligrams. I checked it against the name on the back of my hand twice. I took every bottle on the shelf.

Then I went to the fridge.

It was humming. Somebody, years ago, had put the pharmacy fridge on the backup circuit, probably because of a rule nobody thought about until the end of the world. Inside, it was cold. Rows of white boxes. I found the pens my mom used: the slow one she took at night, and the quick one she took with meals. I'd carried those boxes home from this counter every month for three years. I took all of them, and every pack of needles in the drawer.

Everything went into the backpack, wrapped in the towel.

Then I sat on the floor behind the counter with my back against the drawers and waited for Marcus to come around again. Twenty-six minutes. I'd done the hard part. I'd crossed. I had what I came for.

Eighteen minutes in, I heard something through the wall.

A thin, high sound, like a hinge. It stopped. Then it started again, longer this time.

Out in the court, heads began to turn. Not toward the food court. Toward the store next door.

It was coming from the pet store. And I knew exactly what that whine was.

My daughter named this puppy. If he barks, we all die.

Every Saturday for a year, Lily and I walked to that pet store after her swimming lesson. She'd press her forehead against the glass of his kennel, and he'd press his nose against the other side. I'd stand behind her saying "five more minutes" until the girl at the register laughed at me.

He was supposed to be sold in a week. Then in a month. Then everybody stopped saying anything. Lily said nobody had bought him because he was waiting for us. I said we lived on the tenth floor and Grandma was allergic to everything. Lily said Grandma would get used to it.

Now he was whining through the wall, and the dead were listening.

I got up on my knees and looked over the counter. They were drifting toward the pet store. Not fast. Just leaning that way, one after another, the way they'd leaned toward me under the ceiling. The first ones put their hands on its glass.

And I understood something. That whine was the best thing that could have happened to me.

Every head in the court was turning toward the pet store. Away from the pharmacy door. Away from my sixty steps. If I left him there, the next time Marcus pulled the food court gates, I could walk home with a backpack full of medicine and nobody would even look at me.

He'd keep crying. They'd keep standing at that glass. And sooner or later, he'd stop.

I thought about the gate. About my best friend sitting on the other side of it, and me with the key in my hand, telling myself I didn't have a choice.

I'd had a choice. I'd just picked the one where I got to stay inside.

The pharmacy's stockroom door opened onto the service corridor, exactly like on camera twelve. It was empty and dim. Two doors down was a steel door with a paw-print sticker on it. I used key twenty-seven.

The pet store smelled like sawdust and something worse. The fish tanks were dark. At the back, in the kennel nearest the floor, there he was. Pancake-colored. One ear up and one ear down. A bag of food had fallen against his kennel, and he'd chewed a hole in it through the bars. His water bottle, the big gallon kind clipped to the bars, was almost empty.

He saw me and stopped whining. His whole back end started to wag.

He remembered me. I was the guy who stood behind Lily saying five more minutes.

Leaving him would have saved me. I opened the cage.
