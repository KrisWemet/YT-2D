# Chapter 3 — Part 3

The woman who's been threatening me just saved my life. That's what scares me.

I gave him a handful of food from the torn bag and let him drink from my cupped hand until he stopped shaking. Then I lifted him into the backpack, onto the towel, next to my mom's insulin. He turned around twice and lay down with his chin on the medicine, like he'd been hired to guard it.

I took a red collar off the hook by the register. I don't know why. It just seemed wrong to take him without one.

Back through the corridor. Back into the pharmacy. The crowd was still bunched against the pet store glass, but it was quiet in there now, and they didn't know what to do about it. Some of them were already drifting back.

Then, far away, two pulls on the food court gates. Then four.

I counted to ten. I went.

That's where I was when this started. In my socks, twenty steps out, with eleven pounds of puppy on my back. He pushed his nose through the gap in the zipper. He'd been in a box for a week. Now he could see the sky through the skylights, and a hundred people standing around, and every one of them looked like a new best friend.

He huffed. The girl in the visor stopped tilting her head.

Then he barked.

Just once. High and happy. It was the loudest sound I'd heard in a week. Every head in that court turned toward me at the same time, like a flock of birds.

The girl in the visor took a step. Then the man with the lanyard. Then everyone around the fountain, leaning in. I backed up, and they kept coming, and the pump room door was forty steps away on the other side of them.

Then something hissed at the far end of the hall.

A red light bounced across the floor past the food court, spitting sparks, rolling until it hit a bench. A road flare, the kind I kept in the trunk of my car. It popped and crackled like bacon in a pan, and it was louder than any dog.

Every head turned toward it. And they went, a slow tide of them, back the way they'd come.

I looked up. On the department store's second-floor balcony, a woman was standing at the railing. Long camel coat. A price tag hanging from the sleeve. She lifted one hand, the way you'd wave to somebody across a parking lot. Then she stepped back out of sight.

I walked the last forty steps to the pump room with my heart in my ears. Latch. Ladder. Dark.

Halfway up the column, I switched on my radio.

She was already talking.

A stranger saved my life today. Tonight she sent me the bill.

"There he is," Vera said. Her voice was warm, like we'd run into each other at the grocery store. "That was very brave, Danny. For a dog."

I held on to the ladder and didn't answer.

"I'm not going to pretend I'm not enjoying this," she said. "You've been so careful with me. And now look. You owe me."

I pressed the button. "I didn't ask you to do that."

"Nobody ever asks. That's what makes it a favor." A little laugh. "I'd like to meet. Face to face. I'm tired of talking into a box. Tomorrow at noon. There's a fence on the roof between your side and mine. I'll be at the gate."

I let go of the button. I didn't tell her yes. She didn't need me to.

It took me an hour to get home through the ceiling with a puppy on my back. He didn't make a sound the whole way. Apparently, he only barked in front of a live audience.

Theo was where I'd left him, burning. I woke him up enough to ask the question Marcus had written in capitals.

"Are you allergic to anything?"

"Adults," he mumbled.

I decided that didn't count. I gave him the first capsule with half a bottle of water and wrote the time on the tent wall in marker. Every six hours. I set an alarm on my phone, the only thing it was still good for.

Then I unzipped the backpack.

Pancake climbed out, sniffed the whole tent, and walked straight up Theo's sleeping bag. He lay down across Theo's ankles like he'd been doing it for years.

Theo opened one eye. "You smell like them."

"Sixty steps' worth."

"And you came back with a dog."

"I came back with medicine. The dog was extra."

He looked at the puppy for a long time. Then he closed his eye again. But he left his hand on Pancake's back.

At nine, I went up to the roof. One, two, three, from Lily. One, two, three, from me. Then one, two, three from the department store. Same as every night.

Except tonight, I knew what the woman holding that light looked like. And tomorrow, she wanted to see me up close.

That night, I took the last key off the ring.

I met the woman on the radio face to face. She was perfectly nice. That's the problem.

I didn't take all forty-two keys to meet somebody who wanted one of them. The last key, the one with the red tag, was threaded onto Pancake's new collar, behind the buckle where nobody would look. He was asleep on Theo's feet. Theo's fever was still up. But the red on his leg hadn't moved past the newest line.

I climbed the stockroom ladder at ten to twelve.

The mall roof is one huge flat field of gravel and air vents. Where the department store starts, there's a chain-link fence, eight feet high, with a gate in the middle and a padlock on our side. Walt used to call it the border.

She was already there.

Mid-forties. Hair pinned up. The camel coat, with the price tag still hanging from the cuff. She was holding two paper cups with steam coming off them.

"Danny," she said, smiling like I was late for lunch. "You're taller on the radio."

Behind her stood two men. One was big, maybe fifty, with a gray beard and his hands folded in front of him. He looked at the roof hatch behind me and didn't look away. The other was young and skinny, with a radio on each hip and a tablet under his arm. He was chewing his thumbnail.

"This is Boone," she said. "He doesn't talk. And this is Kip. He doesn't stop."

Kip looked at his shoes.

She pushed one of the cups through a gap by the hinge. Coffee. Real coffee. I hadn't had coffee in nine days. I took it. I hated how good it was.

"How's the dog?" she asked. "Does he have a name?"

"No."

"And the boy? How's his leg?"

I'd never told her about Theo. I'd never told her about anybody.

"Here's what I think," she said. "You have keys. I have people. Boone can carry anything. Kip can fix anything with a battery. Together, this place is a fortress with a food court. Apart, you're a man in a tent with a sick kid and a puppy."

"Where's the girl?" I said.

"Sleeping. She doesn't like heights."

She looked past me, across the highway, at Parkview. She didn't say anything about it. She didn't have to.

Then she looked at my belt. "Walt's keys," she said. "All of them?"

I'd never told her about Walt either.

"All of them," I said. It was forty-one. I didn't correct her.

"Think about it," she said. "Take your time. We've got nothing but."

They walked back across the gravel. Kip went last. At the gate, he reached through to check the padlock, like somebody had asked him to. His fingers pressed something into mine. Then he was gone.

It was a price tag, folded in half. On the back, in tiny handwriting, it said: She'll kill you for that key.

Every night my daughter blinks I love you. Tonight she blinked SOS.

On the morning of day ten, Theo sat up on his own and asked if there were any more of the chocolate protein bars.

I checked his leg. The red had pulled back inside my first line, the one I'd drawn the night before I went out. His forehead was only warm. His eyes were his again.

"You walked on the floor," he said. "In socks."

"Two pairs."

"You're an idiot."

"The book said it was winning."

He looked at Pancake, who was chewing the corner of his blanket. "Did the book say to get a dog?"

"Marcus would have," I said.

It was the first time I'd said Marcus's name to Theo. He didn't ask. He just nodded, like he already knew who that was. He'd been watching me for four months. He probably did.

I read Kip's note again that afternoon. Then I folded it and put it in my shirt pocket, next to Marcus's twenty. I didn't know what to do with either of them.

At five to nine, Theo came up to the roof with me. He carried Pancake inside his sweatshirt, which was a better idea than a backpack.

Across the highway, I found Parkview. Tenth floor. Fourth window from the left. I always count, even when I know where to look.

At nine, the light came on.

Short. Short. Short.

Then long. Long. Long.

Then short. Short. Short.

I'd taught her that when she was five, on the kitchen floor, with a flashlight and a blanket over our heads. S-O-S. For emergencies. She thought it was a game.

She'd never used it. Not once.

Theo was looking at me. "What does that mean?"

I knew exactly what it meant. My mom had gone into that apartment with about ten days of insulin. This was day ten.

I blinked back three times. I love you. I'm here. It was all I had.

For the first time all week, the department store didn't copy her. Its roof stayed dark. Somebody over there had seen it too.

I went downstairs and opened the backpack. The insulin was still there, wrapped in the towel in the coldest corner of the stockroom. Enough for months.

My mom's insulin had run out. The new insulin was in my hand. And there was a mile and a half of the dead between us.
