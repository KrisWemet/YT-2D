# Chapter 3 — Part 1

Right now I'm standing in the middle of a hundred dead people in my socks, and the puppy behind me is about to bark.

He's in my backpack. I can feel him turning circles against my spine, the way dogs do before they lie down. Except he isn't lying down. He's pushing his nose out through the gap in the zipper.

At noon, the skylights put the light straight down on all of us. Nobody's shadow is longer than their shoes.

The man in front of me is still wearing a lanyard. VISITOR, it says. Next to him is a girl in a pretzel-stand visor. Maybe seventeen. Her head is tilted, like she's trying to hear a song from another store.

None of them are looking at me. Yet.

They're all facing the far end of the hall, where my best friend is rattling the food court gates. Two pulls. A pause. Two pulls.

Every time he stops, a few of them start to turn back around.

The pump room door is forty steps ahead of me. The pharmacy is behind me, its glass front still in one piece. I know, because I just walked out of it with everything I went in for. Plus eleven pounds of dog I definitely did not go in for.

I'm wearing two pairs of wool socks. I tried on every boot in my store first. On tile, every single one of them squeaked.

My right boot is lying in the dry fountain, a few feet to my left. It's on its side in the coins, exactly where it fell.

The puppy huffs. It's a tiny sound. The girl in the visor stops tilting her head.

Then I feel him take a breath. A big one. The kind you take before you say something important.

I'd timed this. I'd practiced it. I'd done it for somebody else, which everybody says is the hard part.

And one small dog was about to tell a hundred dead people exactly where I was standing.

A week ago, I'd promised myself I'd never set foot on this floor.

Let me back up.

Every night at nine, my daughter blinks three times at me. Now somebody else blinks back.

The first time it happened, I just stood there on the roof with the flashlight in my hand. One. Two. Three. From the department store roof, aimed straight at the fourth window from the left. Then dark.

I wanted to blink again. Four times. Ten. Anything that would tell Lily which light was mine. But all she had was three blinks for I love you, and the emergency one. I'd never taught her what to do if a stranger joined in.

Theo was standing by the hatch. He'd seen all of it.

"That's your kid," he said. It wasn't a question.

I'd never said her name out loud in my store. I'd been too scared the radio would hear it. I said it then. Lily. Seven. Draws on everything, including walls. Every Saturday she visits the same puppy at the pet store, the one nobody ever buys. She named him Pancake, because he's the color of one.

Theo listened without interrupting. Then he said, "Pancake's a terrible name for a dog."

"I know."

"It's good, though."

It happened again the next night, and the night after that. Three from Lily. Three from me. Three from the department store. Vera never mentioned it on the radio. She asked about my supper. She said the little girl up there with her had a cold. I never asked about the light, and she never brought it up. That was worse than anything she could have said.

Here's where we were on day six.

Water, for once, was fine. Theo and I had been back to the fountain once more, by a better crossing he'd found. Food was the next problem: nineteen protein bars and eleven freeze-dried meals, for two. My mom's insulin had about four days left. Out in the hall, my best friend walked his rounds. Around the fountain, a hundred of them stood waiting for a sound.

And Theo was limping.

He'd limped since the crawl. But now he walked around the tents the long way, so I wouldn't see him put weight on that leg. When I asked, he said it was fine. He said it fast. The same way I used to tell my mom the doors were locked.

The kid who saved my life is burning up, and the medicine is on the other side of a hundred dead people.

On the morning of day seven, Theo didn't get up. I found him curled in the tent nearest the rack with his blanket over his head. The store was warm. He was shivering anyway.

I put the back of my hand on his forehead, the way my mom used to. I took it away fast.

"It's fine," he said, with his eyes shut.

I peeled back the bandage. When I'd cleaned that cut, it was a scrape. Now the skin around it was swollen and shiny, a red patch the size of my palm, hot to the touch. A thin red streak ran up from it toward the back of his knee, like somebody had drawn on him with a marker.

Even I knew that was bad.

The first-aid display had bandages, wipes and a box of painkillers. I gave him two pills and half a bottle of water and watched him fall back asleep with his mouth open. An hour later, the fever was back.

I stood in the middle of the store and did what I always do. I made a list.

He needed antibiotics. The only antibiotics in the building were in the pharmacy, on the far side of the center court. All glass front. On night one, I'd crossed it off my list for exactly that reason.

My mom needed something from the same place. Her insulin would run out in three days. The pharmacy kept it in a fridge behind the counter. I'd picked up her prescriptions there every month for three years.

So the pharmacy had everything I needed. It also had a hundred dead people standing in front of it.

For a week, I'd lived inside this store and above the ceiling. I hadn't set foot on the mall floor since the night I ran here. And even if I made it across, I didn't know what to take. A pharmacy is a thousand little boxes with names nobody can say. Grab the wrong one, and I'd risk everything to bring back nothing.

I didn't know what he needed. But I knew who would have.

To save a kid's life, I had to open my dead best friend's locker.

Marcus kept his nursing textbook in the security office, in the locker next to mine. He studied on his breaks with a highlighter in his teeth. Some nights he quizzed me. I never got one right. He said that made me the perfect patient.

The security office was at the far end of the east wing. On night one, I'd run from there to this store in four minutes. Now there were about a hundred reasons not to run back.

But Theo had told me something the morning I caught him. I go round them.

I knelt by his tent. "Can you get to the security office without touching the floor?"

He opened his eyes. They were glassy. He thought about it for longer than he needed to. Then he reached out of the tent and drew in the chalk with one finger, the way he had for the fountain.

Up the rack. Left at the big duct. Over the shoe store. Along a cable tray above the staff corridor. Down through a hatch by the restrooms.

"Don't touch the silver pipes," he whispered. "They're hot."

"You'll be okay here?"

"I've been okay here for four months."

He meant it as a joke. Neither of us laughed.

I went up alone. It was the first time I'd been in the ceiling without Theo in front of me, telling me where to put my hands. So I told myself, under my breath. Next hand. Now your knee. The steel, never the tiles.

Over the shoe store, a tile shifted under my palm, and I didn't move for a full minute. Nothing underneath me moved either.

The hatch let me down into the staff corridor. The doors at both ends were shut, and it was empty. It smelled like old coffee. It was so quiet I could hear the emergency lights hum.

The security office looked exactly the way I'd left it. My chair pushed back. My jacket on the hook. Lily's drawing still taped to the monitor: MY DAD IS THE KING OF THE MALL. In it, I'm wearing a crown and holding up my keys.

I didn't look at it for long.

Marcus's locker was the one covered in stickers. He never locked it. He said a lock was just a puzzle for a bored guy with time, and the only bored guy in this building was me.

I opened it. His spare uniform shirt was folded on the shelf. Under the shirt was the book. And tucked inside the front cover, folded in half, was a twenty-dollar bill.

My best friend has been dead for a week. Today he saved a kid's life.

There was a sticky note on the twenty, in Marcus's handwriting. Told you I'd pay you back.

I don't know when he wrote it. Maybe he was going to give it to me that night, at one o'clock, right after the joke. Maybe he'd been carrying it around for weeks, waiting for the perfect moment to be annoying about it.

I sat on the floor of the security office with my back against the lockers and held that note for a long time.

He'd sat against my gate and told me he still owed me twenty bucks. He'd said it like the joke was on him. The money had been in here the whole time.

I folded it up and put it in my shirt pocket. There was nothing left in the world to spend it on. But I wasn't leaving it in a locker.

Then I opened the book.

Marcus wrote in the margins of everything. Next to a drawing of the heart, he'd written: big pump, do not break. Next to a page about head injuries: Danny, wear the helmet. On page two-twelve, there was a stick figure asleep in front of a wall of screens. I'm pretty sure it was me.

I found the chapter on skin infections. He'd highlighted half of it.

Red, hot and swollen around a wound. A red streak spreading out from it. Fever. It was all there, in a box with a yellow stripe. The book called it cellulitis. Marcus had drawn a little skull next to the word, then crossed it out and written: not if you're fast.

There was a list of antibiotics. One of them was circled twice. Cephalexin. Five hundred milligrams, four times a day. Under it, in Marcus's handwriting: ALLERGIES FIRST. And under that: Mark the edge of the red with a pen. If it crosses the line, it's winning.

I read that three times. Then I wrote the name on the back of my hand in marker, because I didn't trust myself to remember it.

When I looked up, the wall of screens was looking back at me. The generator was still running the cameras. Every screen was on.

Camera nine was the center court. And at the edge of the picture, its glass front still in one piece, was the pharmacy.

My dead best friend walks the same loop every twenty-six minutes. Tomorrow I'm going to use him.
