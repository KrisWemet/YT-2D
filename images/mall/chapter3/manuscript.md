# The Night Guard
## Chapter Three — The Pharmacy

*Days 6–10 · Maplecrest Mall*

*Complete narration manuscript. Section titles and day/location notes are not spoken. Follows Chapter Two, "The Boy in the Ceiling." American English throughout.*

### 1. In My Socks

*Day 8 · Noon · The center court*

Right now I'm standing in the middle of a hundred dead people in my socks, and the puppy behind me is about to bark.

I can hear him through the glass of the pet store. A thin little whine, like a door that needs oil.

The man closest to me turns his head toward it. He's wearing a lanyard from the phone store. His name tag says KEVIN, HAPPY TO HELP.

Kevin is about four feet away from me.

I'm holding a paper pharmacy bag against my chest so it won't crinkle. Inside it are the pills that are supposed to save a fifteen-year-old's life. Under those, wrapped in a dish towel, are three boxes of insulin for my mom.

My boots are back at the store. I took them off because new boots squeak on tile, and I found that out the hard way.

So I'm in my socks. One of them has a hole in the big toe.

I'm thirty-eight years old.

The whine gets louder.

More heads turn. Not toward me. Toward the pet store. Which would be great news, except I'm standing between them and it.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about walking among the dead. You don't sneak. You stand very still and let them decide you're furniture.

I've been furniture for about forty seconds.

Behind the glass, the puppy puts both paws up on his cage and looks right at me.

I know that puppy.

My daughter named him.

Let me back up.

### 2. A Lovely Light

*Day 6 · Summit Outdoors*

The woman who copied my daughter's signal called the next morning to tell me how pretty it was.

I'd put the battery back in Marcus's radio before I went to sleep. I told myself it was so I'd hear her coming. Really, I think I wanted to know what she'd say.

She waited until breakfast.

"Morning, Danny."

I didn't answer.

"I saw your little light show last night. Very sweet. Three blinks. Is that a family thing?"

Theo was sitting on his bucket, peeling the wrapper off a protein bar one strip at a time. He stopped.

"Not much of a talker today," Vera said. "That's all right. I'll talk. The girl up here slept better last night. I think she likes knowing someone's out there."

There was no girl. Theo had told me. I believed him.

It didn't matter. My stomach still turned over every time she said it.

"Anyway," Vera said. "Offer's open. You've got all those keys. I've got people. Seems a shame to do this alone."

The radio clicked off.

I sat there with it in my hand.

Here's where we were, on day six of thirty. Water: good, for once. Theo and I had carried enough back from the fountain for a few days, and we knew the way to get more. Food: thirty-one protein bars, a shelf of freeze-dried meals, and a camp stove I was scared to light. People in the store: two. Dead in the center court: I'd stopped counting at a hundred.

My mom's insulin: if she'd stretched it, about four days.

I did that math every morning. I didn't do anything with it. There was nothing to do with it. It just sat there, like a bill I couldn't pay.

Theo finished peeling his protein bar.

"She's going to keep calling," he said.

"I know."

"You're going to keep listening."

I took the battery out again and put it in my shirt pocket.

He watched me do it, and he didn't say anything else. Which, for a fifteen-year-old, was a lot of restraint.

### 3. The Cut

*Day 6 · Night*

The kid who saved my life was hiding something under his pant leg.

I noticed because he stopped climbing.

For two days, Theo had treated the storage rack like a staircase. Up to the pipe, through the tile, back down for food. He moved through my store like it was his and I was the one visiting.

On day six, he sat on his bucket all afternoon.

When he got up to go to the bathroom, he put his weight on the rack to stand.

"How's the leg?"

"Fine."

"Can I see it?"

"It's fine."

That's two fines. In my experience, one fine means fine. Two fines means stop asking.

I stopped asking. I'm not proud of that.

At dinner, he didn't finish his half of the freeze-dried lasagna. Theo finished everything. Theo finished the parts of the packet that weren't technically food.

Later, I heard him in his tent, breathing through his teeth.

I took the flashlight and crouched by the flap.

"Theo."

Nothing.

"I'm going to look at it. You can yell at me after."

He didn't yell. That was what scared me.

The bandage I'd put on him two days before was stuck to the skin. When I peeled it back, the cut underneath was puffy and shiny, the color of a sunburn. The red had spread past where the bandage ended. It was hot. I could feel it before I touched it.

"When did it start doing that?"

"Yesterday."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

He looked at the tent wall.

"You'd have to do something about it," he said.

I sat back on my heels.

He'd spent four months up in that ceiling making sure nobody had to do anything about him. One cut wasn't going to change his mind.

I cleaned it again. Put on a fresh bandage. Gave him two of the painkillers from the first-aid display.

Then I took a marker and drew a line on his leg, around the edge of the red.

"What's that for?"

"So we know if it moves."

### 4. A Hundred and Two

*Day 7 · Summit Outdoors*

The thermometer said a hundred and two, and the only medicine that could help was on the other side of a hundred dead people.

By morning, the red had crossed my marker line. The thermometer from the first-aid display said a hundred and two.

By lunchtime, there was a pink streak running up the inside of his leg toward the knee.

I didn't know much about infections. I knew that streak was bad. Everybody knows that streak is bad. It's the one thing every TV doctor points at.

Theo knew it too. He'd stopped pretending to be fine. He just lay in the tent with his eyes half open, shivering in a sleeping bag rated for twenty below.

I went through everything in that store.

Summit Outdoors had seven kinds of bandages. Blister pads. Moleskin. A snakebite kit. Fourteen flavors of electrolyte powder. A survival guide with a chapter called "When Help Is Not Coming."

I read that chapter twice. It said: get help.

There was no antibiotic anywhere. Of course there wasn't. Nobody buys penicillin with their kayak.

The pharmacy was on the far side of the center court. I could see the green cross from my gate if I pressed my face to the bars. It sat between the pet store and the shoe store, and its whole front was glass.

On night one, I'd crossed that court at a run with the keys in my hand. Since then, I'd been above it. I'd been under it. I hadn't once put my feet on that floor.

Nobody had. The floor was theirs.

I sat outside Theo's tent and listened to him breathe.

I want to tell you I decided right then. I didn't. I spent most of that afternoon looking for another answer. I checked the survival guide for a third time. I looked up at the ceiling tile like it might have an idea.

Around four, Theo said something in his sleep. I leaned in.

"Put it down," he mumbled.

It was what I'd said to him the morning I caught him. The first thing I'd ever said to him.

I sat back.

Then I got up and went looking for a second answer, because the first one was walking out there in my socks.

### 5. Marcus's Locker

*Day 8 · Before dawn · The staff corridor*

I opened my dead best friend's locker, and he'd left something for me.

If I was going into that pharmacy, I needed to know what to grab. I didn't know one pill from another. I knew Advil. I knew the pink stuff.

But I knew somebody who'd spent two years studying exactly this.

Marcus kept his nursing books in his locker, because his roommate's dog ate the first one. He told me that story about ten times. Every time, the dog got bigger.

The staff lockers were in the corridor behind the security office, on the upper level. I knew how to get there through the ceiling. On our crawl to the fountain, Theo had pointed it out: left at the cinema instead of right.

I'd never done it without Theo.

I left him a full water bottle, two painkillers, and a note that said BACK SOON in big letters, like he was a houseplant.

Then I climbed the rack.

It was slower alone. Every time a tile shifted, I froze until my arms shook. I said the steps to myself the way Theo said them. Next hand. Now your knee. Don't put your foot where you think the floor is.

It took me forty minutes to go a distance I used to walk in two.

The corridor was quiet. One of them was at the far end, a woman in a food-court visor, standing with her face to the wall. She didn't turn around.

I dropped down beside the lockers in my socks.

Marcus's locker was number fourteen. It had a combination lock. I knew the combination, because Marcus kept forgetting it and writing it on his hand, and one night I told him to just give it to me so he'd stop getting ink on the office phone.

Two-two. One-four. Zero-seven.

His age, his locker, and the number of a quarterback he swore he didn't even like.

The door squeaked. I held it halfway and waited. The woman in the visor stayed where she was.

Inside: a spare uniform shirt. A phone charger. A bag of the pretzel bites he wasn't supposed to bring in. And a textbook as thick as a phone book, with a cracked spine and about a hundred sticky notes poking out of it.

*Fundamentals of Nursing.*

I took it out.

Something slid from between the pages and landed on my foot.

A folded twenty-dollar bill.

There was a sticky note wrapped around it.

### 6. Told You I'd Pay You Back

*Day 8 · The staff corridor*

My best friend had been dead for a week, and he'd just told me how to save a kid's life.

The note said: *DANNY. TOLD YOU I'D PAY YOU BACK. Football is fake. Don't tell anybody I said that.*

I stood there holding it for a long time.

He'd had it ready. He'd folded the twenty and stuck the note on it and put it in his book, and he'd been planning to hand it to me some night as a bit. Some big announcement on the radio, probably. Some joke I'd pretend not to laugh at.

He'd just never gotten around to it.

On night one, on the other side of my gate, the last thing he'd said to me was that he still owed me twenty bucks.

He didn't. He'd already paid me. I just hadn't opened the locker yet.

I put the twenty in my shirt pocket, next to his radio's battery.

Then I sat down on the floor with his book.

Marcus wrote in the margins of everything. Every chapter had his little drawings in it. A stick figure with a stethoscope. A heart with a face. On one page about hand washing, he'd written, *Danny would fail this.*

I laughed out loud. Just once. Then I clapped my hand over my mouth, and the woman in the visor turned her head a little, and I didn't breathe for a while.

The chapter on wound infections had a sticky note on it that said *TEST!!!*

He'd underlined everything. Redness spreading from a wound. Warmth. Fever. A red streak heading toward the body, which the book called a sign the infection was spreading, and which Marcus had circled and labeled *BAD.*

Under that, there was a list of antibiotics used for skin infections. He'd starred one. Cephalexin. Next to it, in his handwriting: *dose is by WEIGHT. Kids are not small adults!!*

There was a whole table about it. I didn't understand half of it. I understood enough to know I needed to bring the book with me, and I needed to read the label.

I closed it and held it against my chest.

For a week I'd been listening to Marcus rattle the gates and thinking, that's what's left of him. That noise.

It wasn't. This was left of him too.

I climbed back up into the ceiling with his textbook tucked down the front of my shirt.

When I got back to the store, Theo had drunk the whole bottle of water. That was the only good news that morning, so I counted it.

### 7. The Rounds

*Day 8 · Summit Outdoors*

To get to the pharmacy, I had to time my walk to my dead best friend's rounds.

There's no sneaking past the dead. But I'd been watching them for a week, and I'd learned a few things.

They follow sound. Anything louder than a whisper turns heads.

When it's quiet, they go back to what they used to do. They stand where they used to stand. The ones in the center court mostly face the food court, like they're waiting in line.

And every forty minutes, Marcus comes around.

He walks the same loop he walked when he was alive, the long way, past every store. Two pulls on every gate. And when he rattles, every head on that side of the court turns toward him.

For a few seconds, every one of them is looking the same way.

That was my window.

I sat with my watch and Marcus's textbook and counted three of his rounds. Forty minutes. Forty-two. Thirty-nine. At noon, his loop would bring him to the far end of the hall, by the department store.

At noon, every face in the center court would turn away from my gate.

There was also the question of my gate.

Steel gates make noise. Marcus was the only one in the building allowed to make that noise anymore. So I'd lift mine while he was rattling his.

I showed Theo the plan. He was awake for that part, mostly.

"You walk heel first," he said.

"What?"

"Like a guard. Heel, then toe. It's loud." He shut his eyes. "Roll your feet. Like you're trying not to wake somebody."

"I've never tried not to wake somebody in my life."

"You have a kid."

That shut me up.

I practiced rolling my feet up and down the store while he watched with one eye. My new boots squeaked on the tile every single step. So I took them off.

At eleven-fifty, I put the battery back in Marcus's radio and set it beside Theo, turned all the way down.

"If I'm not back," I said.

I didn't know how to finish that.

"If I'm not back by nine, go up on the roof and blink at Lily for me. Three times."

He looked at me.

"Come back," he said.

### 8. On the Floor

*Day 8 · 12:00 p.m. · The center court*

I unlocked my own gate and walked out among them.

At two minutes to twelve, I heard it. Far down the hall. Two pulls on a gate. A pause. Two more.

Every head in the center court turned toward the sound.

I lifted my gate two feet. Slowly. With each of Marcus's rattles, I lifted a little more, so his noise covered mine.

Then I rolled under it, and Theo, sweating in his sleeping bag on the floor, pulled it back down behind me.

I was on the floor.

The first thing I noticed was the smell. You'd think a week would make you used to it. It doesn't. It's sweet and wrong, like a dumpster in August.

The second thing was how normal they looked from up close. A teenager in a hoodie from the shoe store. A woman holding a single strap of a purse, like she'd been about to look for her keys. A guy in a suit with one shoe on.

I'd seen most of them before. Not on the cameras. Before. I'd held doors for these people.

I walked.

Roll your feet. Like you're trying not to wake somebody.

The pharmacy was about sixty steps away. I'd counted them from my gate that morning, with my face pressed to the bars. I counted them again now in my head, because counting gave my brain something to do besides screaming.

Eleven. Twelve.

A man in a blue polo stepped backward right into my path. I stopped. He swayed there for a second, close enough that I could see a price sticker on his elbow, from something he must have leaned against.

Then Marcus rattled the next gate, and the man drifted toward the sound.

Twenty. Twenty-one.

Halfway across, I passed the fountain. My boot from day four was still lying at the bottom. Right side up. Like I'd taken it off on purpose.

I didn't stop to look at it, but I wanted to.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine.

Somewhere behind me, Marcus finished the gates on that side. The rattling stopped.

And slowly, the heads started turning back.

### 9. The Green Cross

*Day 8 · The pharmacy*

I came for antibiotics, and I left with my mom's insulin too.

The pharmacy's front door was glass, and it was locked. Key twelve. I'd looked it up on Walt's old key list that morning and wrapped it in tape so it wouldn't jingle against the others.

I got it into the lock on the first try. That has never happened to me once in six years.

The door didn't squeak. Glass doors don't. I've never loved a door so much.

I eased it shut behind me and stood there in the aisle between the cold medicine and the reading glasses, breathing through my mouth.

Through the front glass, the court was turning back to normal. Faces drifting toward the food court again. Nobody facing me.

I went behind the counter.

I'd never been back there. It was very organized. Rows of bottles in alphabetical order, the way I'd always pictured it and never actually seen.

I opened Marcus's book on the counter to the page with the sticky note. Cephalexin.

C.

I found it on the second shelf. Big white bottles with blue labels. I checked the name against Marcus's handwriting three times, letter by letter, like a man defusing a bomb. Then I checked it a fourth time. Then I put two bottles in the paper bag.

The book said to take it for days, not hours. The book also said a lot of other things about weight and doses that I was going to read very carefully when I wasn't standing in a glass box in the middle of a hundred dead people.

I was about to leave when I saw the fridge.

It was a tall glass-front refrigerator at the back of the pharmacy, still humming. The mall's generator runs the emergency lights, the cameras and the freezers. Somebody, years ago, had decided this fridge counted.

Inside were vaccines. Eye drops. And insulin. Rows of it.

I stood with my hand on the fridge door.

My mom's insulin was going to run out in about two days. Maybe less, if she'd been honest with herself, which she never was about her own health.

She was a mile and a half away. Across eight lanes of the dead.

I had no way to get anything to her. I knew that.

I took three boxes anyway. Her brand. I recognized the orange label, because I'd picked it up for her every month for four years.

I wrapped them in a dish towel from the break room so they'd stay cool and wouldn't rattle.

Then I picked up the bag, held it against my chest, and opened the front door.

That's when I heard the whine.

### 10. Pancake

*Day 8 · The pet store*

My daughter named this puppy, and if he barked, we were both dead.

The pet store was right next door. Its gate was only half down. Somebody had been closing up on night one and hadn't finished.

Behind the glass, in a cage at the front of the store, a puppy was standing on his back legs, whining at me.

Small. Brown and white. One ear up, one ear flat. He'd been there since September.

Every Saturday, before I went home to sleep, I met my mom and Lily at the mall entrance. Before we did anything else, we went to see him. Lily would sit on the floor in front of his cage with her knees pulled up and tell him about her week.

She named him Pancake, because his flat ear looked like one.

Nobody ever bought Pancake. Lily said it was because he was waiting for us. I said it was because of the ear.

Now he was thinner. Somebody had left a bag of kibble against the side of the cage, and he'd chewed through it from inside. The water bottle was almost empty.

He'd been alone in there for a week, listening to all of them.

And now he'd seen a person.

The whine went up.

Kevin from the phone store turned. Then the woman with the purse strap. Then six or seven more. Not fast. They never hurry. They just started to drift, one at a time, toward the pet store glass.

Toward me.

I was standing right in front of it. In my socks. With a paper bag.

If I walked home right now, the whine would pull them all to the pet store. They'd crowd that glass. They'd forget I existed.

Walking home would work. That's the thing. It would have worked fine.

Pancake put his nose between the bars and looked at me like Lily did when she knew I was about to say no.

I thought about telling her. Someday. Across a kitchen table, if I ever got to sit at one with her again.

*I left him.*

I ducked under the half-closed gate.

### 11. It Goes Loud

*Day 8 · The pet store*

I was about to die in a pet store, and then somebody threw a light.

The cage had a simple latch. No key. The one door in the mall that didn't need one.

I opened it and Pancake came straight into my arms like he'd been practicing. He was shaking. So was I.

I got him inside my jacket against my chest. He was warm and he smelled terrible, and his heart was going so fast I could feel it through my shirt.

"Shh," I whispered. "Shh, buddy. Please."

For one second, it worked.

Then he got his head out of my collar, saw the faces pressed to the glass, and barked.

Just once. A tiny bark. It sounded like a gunshot.

Every head in the center court snapped toward us.

They came.

Not running. They don't run. But they were all coming at once now, from every direction, and they leaned on the pet store glass the way the crowd leaned on my gate the night Marcus died. All together. Like the doors on Black Friday.

The glass creaked. A crack ran across it from one corner, quiet as a pencil line.

I backed into the store. Fish tanks. Dog beds. A display of chew toys shaped like hot dogs. There was a door at the back that said STAFF ONLY. Locked. I tried three keys. None of them.

I had Pancake in one arm, the bag in the other, and no free hand for a fourth key.

The crack in the glass got longer.

That's when I heard a hiss from somewhere far down the hall.

A red light came spinning out from the upper level of the department store and bounced across the floor toward the escalators. Sputtering and hissing and throwing red smoke.

A road flare.

It skittered to a stop beside the escalator, at least two hundred feet away.

Every head in the court turned toward it.

Then they went.

All of them. Drifting away from the glass, toward the noise and the red light, like a crowd heading for fireworks.

I didn't wait to understand it. I went back under the gate and walked behind them, rolling my feet, with my jacket buttoned over a puppy and my hand pressed over his face.

At my gate, I knocked twice.

Theo was there. He must have been watching through the bars the whole time. With a fever of a hundred and two, he got his fingers under that gate and lifted it.

I rolled under. He let it down.

We lay on the floor of Summit Outdoors, breathing.

Pancake climbed out of my jacket, walked across my chest, and licked Theo's face.

Theo looked at the dog. Then at me.

"Seriously?" he whispered.

### 12. Now You Owe Me

*Day 8 · Afternoon · Summit Outdoors*

The woman who'd been threatening me had just saved my life, and that scared me more than anything.

First, the pills.

I read Marcus's table four times. I weighed Theo on a bathroom scale from the fitness aisle, which he did not enjoy. I checked the label. I checked the book. I checked the label again.

He swallowed the first dose with electrolyte water that tasted like a blue crayon.

Then I sat beside his tent with the book open on my knees, and I read the chapter on wound infections all the way through, including every one of Marcus's jokes, while Pancake slept on Theo's feet.

At four o'clock, I put the battery back in the radio.

She was waiting.

"Danny. Hello. You looked very brave out there."

I didn't say anything.

"In your socks. That was a nice touch. I did wonder about the dog."

She'd seen me. All of it. From the department store balcony, maybe. Or somewhere else. I didn't know how she was seeing things. I didn't like how many questions that answer opened.

I pressed the button.

"Why?"

"Why what?"

"Why throw it?"

"Because you were about to get eaten in a pet store, and that seemed like a waste." She paused. "Also, I'm not a monster. Whatever you've decided I am."

Theo was awake. He was watching me from the tent.

I wanted to say it. I wanted to tell her I knew about the drill. That I knew what had brought them into the mall, and who'd kept it running, and who'd been standing at my gate when it happened.

I didn't. If she knew what I knew, she'd want to know who told me.

And there was only one person in the mall who could have.

"Thank you," I said. It came out like a cough.

"You're welcome." I could hear her smiling. "Now you owe me, Danny."

"What do you want?"

"Just to talk. Face to face. I think we'd get along, you and me. The roofs touch, you know. There's a fence between them."

"When?"

"Tomorrow. Five o'clock. Come alone. I'll bring a couple of friends, but they're very polite."

She clicked off.

I took the battery out and put it back in my pocket, next to the twenty.

"Don't go," Theo said.

"She saved my life."

"So she wants something."

He was fifteen and running a fever, and he was still the smartest person in the building.

### 13. The Fence

*Day 9 · 5:00 p.m. · The roof*

I finally met the woman on the radio, and she was wearing the coat.

The roof of Summit Outdoors runs right into the roof of the department store. Between them, there's a chain-link fence about eight feet high, with a padlocked gate in the middle. None of my keys fits it. I checked.

I climbed up at ten to five and stood about six feet back from the fence.

They came out of the department store's roof door right on time.

Three of them.

The first one was a big man in his fifties, gray beard, wearing a canvas work jacket. He didn't look at me. He looked at the roof around me, the hatch, the corners, like he was counting exits. He didn't say a word the whole time. I started thinking of him as the wall.

The second one was a kid. Maybe twenty-four. Skinny, glasses, a backpack full of cables. He kept fiddling with a radio antenna and looking at the ground.

The third one was Vera.

She was in her forties. Dark hair pulled back. A nice wool coat, the color of oatmeal.

There was still a price tag hanging from the sleeve.

"Danny," she said. She sounded genuinely happy to see me, like an old friend at a reunion. "You're taller than you look on camera."

On camera.

"Vera," I said.

"Thank you for coming. I know you didn't want to." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "So. Let me just say it. You've got forty-two keys. Walt's keys. And I've got people who can carry things. Food court freezers. The grocery stockroom. The pharmacy, properly, not one bag at a time."

She knew Walt's name. I'd never told her Walt's name.

"We'd be safer together," she said. "That's not a trick. That's just math. You're good at math, I think."

"Where's the girl?"

She didn't blink.

"Sleeping. She's had a hard week. I'll introduce you when she's up to it."

"I'd like to see her."

"And you will." She smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part. "Think about it. You don't have to answer today."

She turned to go. The wall turned with her.

The kid with the cables stayed a second longer. He stepped up to the fence like he was checking the padlock.

Then he pushed a folded piece of paper through the chain-link and walked away without looking at me.

I waited until the roof door shut.

Then I picked it up.

It was a receipt from the department store. On the back, in pencil, very small:

*She'll kill you for that key.*

### 14. Short, Short, Short

*Day 10 · Nine o'clock*

Every night, my daughter blinks I love you. Tonight, she blinked SOS.

Theo's fever broke on the morning of day ten.

I knew because I woke up and he was sitting up in his tent eating a protein bar, and Pancake was sitting in front of him with the most dignified expression a dog has ever had, waiting for crumbs.

The red on his leg had pulled back inside my marker line.

"Hey," Theo said, with his mouth full.

"Hey."

"The dog snores."

"So do you."

He didn't argue. That's how I knew he really felt better.

I spent that afternoon reading Kip's note over and over. That was his name. Theo had heard Vera call him that on night one. Kip. The kid with the cables.

She'll kill you for that key.

I knew which key. The red tag. B3. Walt's warning. The thing Vera's crew had drilled through a wall to get to on the night everything ended.

She'd smiled at me through a fence and asked me to share.

At ten to nine, I climbed to the roof with my flashlight. Theo came with me this time. He was still limping, but he wanted to see. Pancake stayed downstairs with a chew toy shaped like a hot dog.

I found Parkview against the dark. Tenth floor. Fourth window from the left.

At nine, her light came on.

Short. Short. Short.

I started to lift my flashlight.

Then: long. Long. Long.

I stopped.

Short. Short. Short.

Then the light went off, and came back, and did it again.

Theo looked at me.

"That's not three," he said.

No. It wasn't three.

It was the other signal. The one I'd taught her at the kitchen table, tapping it on her hand with my finger. The one I'd told her was a game. The one I'd hoped every night she would never, ever need.

S. O. S.

I knew what it meant. I'd been doing the math every morning for ten days.

My mom's insulin was gone.

I reached into my jacket. The three boxes were still there, wrapped in the dish towel, exactly where I'd put them in the pharmacy.

I had what she needed in my hand.

It was a mile and a half away, across eight lanes of the dead.

How do you get something across a highway that nobody can cross?
